Parameterized quantum circuits (PQCs) represent a central class of controllable quantum evolutions, yet their trainability can be severely limited by exponentially vanishing gradients, known as the barren plateau phenomenon. Existing analyses typically rely on strong randomness assumptions, such as unitary designs and remain limited in addressing general structured circuits.

\emph{Introduction}.
Quantum technology has gradually come to play an indispensable role in a wide range of practical applications~\cite{daley2022practical, huang2022quantum, bravyi2018quantum, lee2023evaluating, arvidsson2020quantum}, particularly in frontier fields such as metrology, precision measurement, and quantum machine learning~\cite{liu2024optimal,munoz2024photonic,de2025improving,yang2025programmable,haug2022natural,miao2024neural,tilly2022variational,wu2023quantumdarts}. In the current Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era, hardware imperfections and limited coherence times hinder the execution of complex quantum computation. To achieve quantum advantage under these constraints, Variational Quantum Algorithms (VQAs) have emerged as the dominant paradigm~\cite{peruzzo2014variational,farhi2014quantum,mcclean2016theory,o2016scalable,kandala2017hardware,grimsley2019adaptive,endo2020variational,cimini2024variational}. VQAs constitute a hybrid quantum-classical framework that solves complex problems by optimizing trainable PQCs via classical algorithms (e.g., gradient descent~\cite{mitarai2018quantum,schuld2019evaluating}). Therefore, the successful execution of VQAs is highly dependent on the trainability of the chosen PQC. The trainability of a PQC is typically directly governed by the gradient variance of the cost function. When the gradient variance of the cost function vanishes exponentially with the system size, the average gradient concentrates around zero. This means that large-scale circuits with this property often require an exponentially large number of measurements to converge, leading to severe trainability issues. This phenomenon, known as the barren plateau~\cite{mcclean2018barren}, constitutes a central obstacle to achieving quantum advantage in the NISQ era.

To investigate the factors influencing the trainability of PQCs, the unitary 2-design framework is commonly used to analyze the gradient variance of a given PQC at a specific parameter~\cite{ortiz2021entanglement, holmes2022connecting, gong2024enhancing, arrasmith2021effect, qi2023barren, liu2022estimating,cunningham2025investigating}. The unitary 2-design framework is essentially a simulation of the Haar random distribution, relying on the assumption that the analyzed PQC can approximate the uniform Haar measure to some extent. However, PQCs that approximate the Haar measure exhibit strong randomness~\cite{holmes2022connecting}, which results in poor trainability and a lack of practical utility.

In practice, quantum circuits are typically designed with tailored structures for specific problems, which inevitably leads to a lack of randomness. Common examples include shallow or locality-constrained hardware-efficient ansatzes~\cite{watanabe2023optimizing}, circuits generated by continuous-time evolution under structured Hamiltonians~\cite{park2024hamiltonian}, and symmetry-constrained structures~\cite{lee2018generalized}. Such non-random PQCs may exhibit strong trainability and play an important role in practical applications. However, existing unitary 2-design frameworks are inherently tied to the strong randomness assumption; they are effectively restricted to analyzing untrainable circuits, leaving these practical, structured (and potentially trainable) circuits outside their scope. Many studies have attempted to extend the current analytical frameworks. For example, Refs. \cite{brandao2016local,cerezo2021cost,pesah2021absence,ragone2024lie} extended the unitary 2-design framework to analyze PQCs composed of multiple unitary 2-design blocks, but each block remains constrained by the strong randomness assumption. The work in Ref.~\cite{anschuetz2024unified} sought to develop a more general analytical approach based on Jordan algebra decomposition. Nevertheless, these frameworks typically rely on prior knowledge of the circuit's underlying mathematical structure, which is often difficult to determine. To systematically analyze the trainability of practical PQCs, it is necessary to propose a more comprehensive gradient variance framework applicable to a broader class of PQCs, and within which the mathematical structure behind a given circuit can be obtained straightforwardly.

In this Letter, we develop a measure-based framework to characterize the trainability of PQCs beyond the standard unitary design paradigm. This framework approximates the distribution of any PQC to arbitrary precision by developing a class of polynomial probability measures, and obtains explicit gradient variance expressions by decomposing this distribution using zonal polynomials and Weingarten calculus. We also establish a level classification for trainability by projecting the gradient variance into different orthogonal subspaces, which reveals the significant advantages of the previously overlooked unitary 1-design in terms of trainability. Furthermore, we introduce the concept of "improvement potential" to quantify the maximal variance enhancement achievable under local perturbations. Our work provides systematic analytical guidance for identifying and enhancing the trainability of parameterized quantum circuits in the NISQ era.

Structurally, a circuit $U(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ consists of a sequence of parameterized quantum gates, whose tunable parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ control the circuit’s evolution. \begin{proposition}
Let $U(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \prod_{l=1}^{L} U_l(\theta_l) W_l$ be a parameterized quantum circuit composed of fixed entangling gates $\{W_l\}$ and parameter-dependent rotation gates $\{U_l(\theta_l) := \exp(-i P_l \theta_l)\}$, where each $P_l$ is a Pauli word and the parameters $\theta_l$ are i.i.d.\ uniformly distributed on $[0,2\pi)$. 
For a Pauli word $P$, define
\begin{equation}
    P_1 := I, \quad 
    P_2 := P, \quad 
    P_3 := \frac{I + iP}{\sqrt{2}}, \quad 
    P_4 := \frac{I - iP}{\sqrt{2}}.
\end{equation}
Then the following statements hold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Parameter-dependent rotation gate.}  
    The probability distribution of a parameter-depedent rotation gate $U_l(\theta_l)$ is represented by the polynomial
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
            1 + \frac{1}{2} \sum_{k=1}^2
            Z_{(1,0,\cdots,0,-1),\,P_{l,k}} + \frac{1}{4} 
            \sum_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in S_2}
            \sum_{k=1}^{4}
            Z_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\,P_{l,k}}.
        \end{aligned}
        \label{eq:single}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Fixed entangling gate.}  
     The probability distribution of a fixed entangling gate $W_l$ is represented by the Dirac distribution concentrated at $W_l$.

    \item \textbf{Full circuit.}  
    The probability distribution of the full circuit $U(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ is represented by the polynomial
    \begin{equation}
        \begin{aligned}
            1 
            + \frac{1}{2^{L}} 
            &\sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_L=1}^{2} 
            Z_{(1,0,\ldots,0,-1),\,\prod_{s=1}^{L} P_{s,i_s}W_s}\\
            + \frac{1}{4^{L}} 
            &\sum_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in S_2}
            \sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_L=1}^{4} 
            Z_{\boldsymbol{\lambda},\,\prod_{s=1}^{L} P_{s,i_s} W_s}.
        \end{aligned}
     \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
Here, “represented by the polynomial’’ means that, when computing gradient variances, one may equivalently replace the probability measure of the circuit with the polynomial measure associated with the above polynomial.
\label{prop:comp}
\end{proposition}
Proposition~\ref{prop:comp} provides an explicit polynomial measure family to approximate any PQC under the hardware-efficient ansatz. \emph{Level classification of variances.}
The above analysis shows that an arbitrary PQC can be approximated by polynomial probability measures. The balanced component of such measures, which governs the gradient variance, admits a decomposition in terms of zonal polynomials~\cite{james1961zonal,james1964distributions,constantine1963some}. Consequently, the total variance can be expressed as the sum of three contributions:
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Var}^{(\mu_L, \mu_R)}(\rho, O) = \sum_{\alpha=0}^2 \operatorname{Var}_\alpha^{(\mu_L, \mu_R)}(\rho, O),
    \label{eq:var_decomp}
\end{equation}
where the $\alpha$-th order variance is defined by
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \operatorname{Var}_\alpha^{(\mu_L, \mu_R)}(\rho, O)&:=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in S_
        \alpha}\int\dif\mu_L(U_L)\dif\mu_R(U_R)\\
        &\left(\int\dif\mu_H(V)\overline{Z_{\lambda,V}(U)}\operatorname{Tr}[V\rho V^\dagger X_L]^2\right).
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:nth-var}
\end{equation}

Through the zonal polynomial decomposition, we have projected the variance onto several subspaces that admit explicit computation successfully. Among these, the bound $\operatorname{Var}_0 \leq C_0(\rho,O) d^{-2}, C_0(\rho,O)>0$ for the zeroth-order variance is a well-established result~\cite{mcclean2018barren}. For the first- and second-order variances, we provide the following theoretical upper bounds.
\begin{theorem}
    Let $U_L$ and $U_R$ be the unitaries obtained from the splitting defined before, with $U_L\sim \mu_L$ and $U_R\sim \mu_R$. Then the $\alpha$-th-order variance admits the following asymptotic bounds:
    \begin{equation}
        {\rm Var}^{(\mu_L,\mu_R)}_\alpha(\rho, O) \leq C_\alpha(\rho, O)d^{\alpha-2}\quad {\rm for}\ \alpha=0,1,2,
    \end{equation}
    where $C_\alpha(\rho, O)$ is a constant independent on $d$.
    \label{thm:1}
\end{theorem}
Building on the uniform approximation by polynomial measures, Theorem~\ref{thm:1} characterizes the variance contributions from orthogonal subspaces $\{\oplus_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in S_\alpha} V_\alpha:\alpha = 0, 1,2 \}$ for general PQCs, thereby providing a level classification of total variances based on structural properties of measures. It demonstrates that the second-order variance determines whether there exists a barren plateau at the given parameter. Moreover, for a pure state $\rho$, we can further build a relation between the total variance and the first-order variance by
    \begin{equation}
        {\rm Var}^{(\mu_L,\mu_R)}(\rho, O) \leq \frac{d}{2}{\rm Var}^{(\mu_L,\mu_R)}_1(\rho, O) + \mathcal{O}(d^{-1}),
        \label{eq:upper_bound}
    \end{equation}
indicating that a large first-order variance is necessary to avoid degraded trainability. Readers can refer to the Supplementary Materials Sec.~\RomanNumeralCaps{4} for detailed proofs of Theorem~\ref{thm:1} and Eq.~\eqref{eq:upper_bound}.

By Eq.~\eqref{eq:nth-var}, the first-order contribution to the variance vanishes if at least one of $\mu_L$ or $\mu_R$ forms a unitary $1$-design. It then follows from Eq.~\eqref{eq:upper_bound} that the total variance is bounded by $O(d^{-1})$. The following proposition establishes that this bound is tight.
\begin{proposition}
   Fix a pure state $\rho$ and an idempotent operator $P$ on $\mathbb{C}^d$ (i.e., $P^2 = I$). For $m \in \mathbb{N}$, define a probability measure $\nu_m$ on $\mathcal{U}(d)$ by
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\dif \nu_m}{\dif \mu_H}(U)
    = c_m \, \bigl(\operatorname{Tr}[U \rho U^\dagger P]\bigr)^{2m},
    \label{eq:1-design}
\end{equation}
where $c_m > 0$ is the normalization constant. Then the following statements hold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The measure $\nu_m$ forms a unitary $1$-design.
    \item Moreover, for sufficiently large $m$, there exist probability measures $\mu_L$ and $\mu_R$ on $\mathcal{U}(d)$, and observables $O_1, O_2$, such that
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{Var}^{(\mu_L, \nu_m)}(\rho, O_1),\ \mathrm{Var}^{(\nu_m, \mu_R)}(\rho, O_2)\;\geq\; \gamma d^{-1},
    \end{equation}
    where $\gamma > 0$ is a constant independent of $d$.
\end{enumerate}
\label{prop:1-design}
\end{proposition}
This result indicates that when the probability measure of one side of the circuit at a parameter forms a unitary 1-design, its total gradient variance can be at most on the order of \(d^{-1}\), thereby exhibiting superior trainability compared to unitary 2-designs, which are bounded by the $d^{-2}$ scaling. The proofs of Theorem~\ref{thm:1} and Proposition~\ref{prop:1-design} are provided in the Supplementary Material, Sec.~\RomanNumeralCaps{4}.

\emph{The Improvement Potential.}
Beyond characterizing the trainability of a given PQC, it is also of interest to understand how to enhance trainability within a fixed circuit architecture. So far, several methods have been developed to enhance trainability, such as the substitution by shallow circuits, tailored problem-specific ansatz, and optimized initialization strategies~\cite{zhang2024absence,cerezo2021cost, liu2024mitigating, grant2019initialization,skolik2021layerwise,verdon2019learning}. However, these approaches typically rely on global or heuristic modifications and provide limited quantitative guidance within a fixed circuit structure. Here, we propose a formula to characterize the maximum improvement in trainability by inserting parametric oracles at a given position. Specifically, we insert a parametric oracle $S_p$ associated with the probability measure $\mu$ on the right side of $U_R$. The probability measure for the combination of $U_L$ and $S_p$ is the convolution $\mu_L * \mu$. The schematic diagram of the process is shown in Figure~\ref{fig: circuit}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{figures/circuit.pdf}
\caption{\label{fig: circuit} The process of inserting a parametric oracle at a given position to potentially enhance trainability. Here, $S_p$ denotes an arbitrary parametric unitary oracle associated with probability measure $\mu$ and the improvement potential $k(\mu_L,\mu_R)$ represents the maximal improvement of the total variance during the process.}
\label{fig:insert}
\end{figure}
Formally, we consider the following quantity
\begin{equation}
    k(\mu_L,\mu_R) : = \sup_{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{U}(d))}\frac{{\rm Var}^{(\mu_L*\mu, \mu_R)}(\rho, O)}{{\rm Var}^{(\mu_L, \mu_R)}(\rho, O)},
\end{equation}
which characterizes the maximal relative enhancement of variance at the parameter $\theta_\ell$ by inserting an oracle. If $k(\mu_L,\mu_R)=1$, the PQC exhibits no improvement potential at $\theta_\ell$; whereas $k(\mu_L,\mu_R)>1$ indicates the existence of improvement potential at that parameter. We provide the following cri terion to determine whether $k(\mu_L,\mu_R)>1$ or not:
\begin{theorem}
For a PQC at $\theta_\ell$ with the probability measure pair $(\mu_L, \mu_R)$, the improvement potential obtained by inserting a parametric oracle at $\theta_\ell$ is
\begin{equation}
    k(\mu_L,\mu_R)  = \frac{\|h*g\|_\infty}{\|hg\|_1}.
\end{equation}
Here $g$ is the Radon–Nikodym derivative $\dif\mu_L/\dif\mu_H$ and $h$ represents the variance obtained after integrating over the right unitary defined by
\begin{equation}
    h(V) = \int\dif\mu_R(U_R)\operatorname{Tr}[V \rho V^\dagger i[U_R\rho U_R^\dagger, H_\ell]]^2.
\end{equation}
Both functions can be obtained by the pair $(\mu_L,\mu_R)$.
\label{thm:potential}
\end{theorem}
Moreover, we find that the quantity ${\rm Var}^{(\mu_L, \mu_H)}(\rho, O)$ provides a reference threshold. This threshold can be used to assess whether improvement is possible at a given parameter. For a pair of probability measures $(\mu_L, \mu_R)$, if ${\rm Var}^{(\mu_L, \mu_R)}(\rho, O) < {\rm Var}^{(\mu_H, \mu_R)}(\rho, O)$, then we can prove that the circuit must possess improvement potential at $\theta_\ell$. In contrast, for circuits where $\mu_R$ satisfies the unitary 2-design property, the gradient variance satisfies ${\rm Var}^{(\mu_L, \mu_R)}(\rho, O) = {\rm Var}^{(\mu_H, \mu_R)}(\rho, O)$. In this case, such circuits exhibit no training potential, i.e., $k(\mu_L, \mu_R) = 1$. Therefore, all parameterized quantum circuits, after being optimized through the process illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:insert}, can achieve performance at a given parameter position that is no worse than that of unitary 2-design circuits. When $\operatorname{Var}^{(\mu_L, \mu_R)}(\rho, O) \geq \operatorname{Var}^{(\mu_H, \mu_R)}(\rho, O)$, no definitive conclusion can be drawn from this comparison alone. In this regime, the presence or absence of improvement potential must be determined using the criterion established in Theorem~\ref{thm:potential}. More detailed discussions and constructive examples are provided in the Supplementary Material Sec.~\RomanNumeralCaps{5}.

\emph{\label{sec:5} Connection to classical theories and Applications}.
In the final part of this paper, we present three examples to validate our theory and illustrate potential applications to the characterization of trainability.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(i)] \textbf{Equivalence with direct integration over parameter distributions.}
    Computing the gradient variance via integration with zonal polynomials in Proposition~\ref{prop:comp} is equivalent to direct integration over the parameter distribution. As an illustrative example, consider $U_R = \exp(-i P \theta)$, where $P$ is a Pauli word and $\theta$ satisfies the uniform distribution on $[0,2\pi)$. Then both the integral in Eq.~\eqref{eq:single} and the direct integration over $\theta$ yield the same gradient variance: $\frac{1}{4}\sum_{k=1}^4 \operatorname{Tr}\!\left[P^k \rho P^k X_L\right]^2$.
    This agreement validates the correctness of our framework.

    \item[(ii)] \textbf{Approximation of classical unitary design theory.}
    Any probability measure $\mu$ that forms a unitary $2$-design can be approximated by a sequence of polynomial probability measures of the form
    \begin{equation}
        \left\{
        \nu \;:\;
        \frac{\dif \nu}{\dif \mu_H}
        =
        1 + \sum_{|\lambda_+| > 2} \sum_{V_\lambda}
        a_{\lambda, V_\lambda} \, Z_{\lambda, V_\lambda}
        \right\},
    \end{equation}
    where the coefficients $a_{\lambda, V_\lambda}$ are chosen to ensure positivity and normalization.

    \item[(iii)] \textbf{A practical criterion for trainability.}
    By Eq.~\ref{eq:upper_bound}, if the scalar
    \[
        d \cdot \mathrm{Var}_1^{(\mu_L,\mu_R)}(\rho, O)
    \]
    decays faster than any polynomial in $\log_2(d)$, then a barren plateau occurs. Under the common assumption that all parameters are i.i.d. and uniformly distributed on $[0, 2\pi)$, one has
    \begin{equation}
        d \cdot \mathrm{Var}_1^{(\mu_L,\mu_R)}(\rho, O)
        \;\approx\;
        2\,\operatorname{Tr}\!\left[U \rho^2 U^\dagger X_L^2\right].
    \end{equation}
   This quantity can be efficiently estimated by replacing each rotation gate $\exp(-i P_j \theta_j)$ with a uniformly random choice from the set $\{P_j^1, P_j^2, P_j^3, P_j^4\}$, while keeping the entangling gates unchanged. Similar circuit constructions can be found in Ref.~\cite{arute2019quantum}.
\end{enumerate}

In contrast, our theoretical results remain valid and accurate for all values of $m$, which are currently beyond the reach of numerical simulations.

\emph{Conclusion and discussion}.
Trainability is a key metric for evaluating the performance of VQAs, as it determines whether the circuit parameters of the underlying PQCs can be efficiently updated to a desirable solution. In this Letter, we developed a measure-theoretic framework based on balanced polynomial measures to characterize the trainability of arbitrary PQCs. Through zonal polynomial decomposition, this framework yields explicit expressions for the gradient variance and proposes a level classification system for trainability. Our work breaks through the strong randomness assumptions of the existing unitary design framework, enabling the trainability analysis of highly structured, practically meaningful PQCs and thereby paving the way for circuit design on near-term quantum hardware.

Looking ahead, it would be interesting to relate this framework to the decomposition of circuit-accessible spaces via dynamic Lie algebras~\cite{diaz2023showcasing,fontana2024characterizing,ragone2024lie}, thereby developing a more physics-informed perspective. In addition, the framework is well-suited to the study of circuits with symmetries~\cite{lee2018generalized,schatzki2024theoretical,marvian2024theory}, which we leave for future work. Finally, our approach can be extended to other ansatz, such as the unitary coupled cluster ansatz~\cite{taube2006new,filip2020stochastic,anand2022quantum} and the Hamiltonian variational ansatz~\cite{wecker2015progress,wiersema2020exploring}.
